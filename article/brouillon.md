# Earned Autonomy for AI Agents: A Pre-registered Error-Budget Test of Per-Action-Type Delegation

**Otsoa Dubief**, independent researcher, otsoa.dubief@gmail.com. ORCID [0009-0002-7322-7575](https://orcid.org/0009-0002-7322-7575)

Preregistration: https://osf.io/vycge/ (frozen at git tag `gel-2026-11-09`). Priority record: https://osf.io/7ju3c/.

---

## Abstract

Which actions may an AI agent take without human sign-off? We propose an open rule: an agent earns autonomy on one action type after N consecutive verified successes and loses it at the first confirmed error; N is derived from an error budget fixed in advance per stakes level by adapting the unrestricted AOQL of continuous sampling plans, with a conditional bound on the extra errors delegation lets through. We test it in a pre-registered replay: three Claude 5.5 models on four public benchmarks (τ-bench airline, retail and banking; WorkBench), 14,266 episodes played (13,623 analysed), writes checked by exact comparison (98.7% coverage) and alerts reviewed on blind sheets by separate Claude instances. No configuration reached the pre-registered level of evidence required for delegation (verdict R4). Only one of twelve configurations had enough volume to be judged; its error rate, 8.6% (lower bound 4.1%), is established above the 0.76% threshold. Of its 79 errors, 48 are one plot end-date discrepancy reviewers decided both ways; lifting it throughout leaves 31, established by two errors, and lifting also four one-day window shifts would not. The other eleven had too little effective volume to conclude either way. Descriptively (no multiplicity correction), 20 of 40 model–environment–type combinations have an error rate established above their threshold (11 of them on banking); 13 show no observed error but too little volume. The rule was not refuted, but it was hardly put to the test, and its usefulness could not be judged. These rates count action errors confirmed by review against a single expected path per task; in a blind sample reviewed by the author, 3 of 10 confirmed alerts were lifted, too few to rule out false-alert shares that would overturn the judged excess. As the plan stated in advance, earning autonomy under such budgets takes far more verified actions than these benchmarks provide at our volumes.

## 1 Introduction

An agent that books flights, refunds orders or files calendar entries takes dozens of actions per task. Most are reads. Some change the state of the world: a reservation is modified, money moves, an e-mail leaves. The practical question that every deployment faces is simple to state: *which of these actions can the agent take alone, and on what evidence?*

Current practice answers it in one of three ways. A fixed allow-list grants autonomy to action categories judged safe in advance. A risk triage looks at each action's stakes, without any record of how the agent has behaved. Or the operator approves more and more actions as trust builds, without a stated criterion. None of these ties the decision to the agent's observed record on that kind of action, and none says how many errors the arrangement will let through.

We study an alternative inspired by industrial quality control. In a continuous sampling plan (Dodge, 1943, p. 265), every unit is inspected until i consecutive units are found conforming; inspection then drops to a fraction, and returns to 100 % at the first defect. Read in reverse for agents, this becomes: validate every action of a type until the agent has N consecutive verified successes on it, then let the agent act alone on that type, and withdraw autonomy at the first error. The quality-control literature also gives the key guarantee. Under arbitrary, non-stationary quality, the outgoing defect rate of such a plan is bounded (the "unrestricted" AOQL; Lieberman, 1953, pp. 480-482; Derman, Johns and Lieberman, 1959, pp. 1176-1177). Applied to agents, a simple cycle-counting argument gives a bound on the extra errors that delegation lets through, which does not assume the agent is stable. That matters, because agents change: the provider updates the model, the prompt is edited, a tool is added.

**Contributions.** This paper makes three contributions.

1. **A rule with a stated guarantee.** We give a complete, open rule for earned autonomy *per action type* (Section 3). Its key parameter, the run length N, is not tuned on data. It is derived from an error budget fixed in advance for each stakes level and from the expected mix of actions in each environment. Under immediate detection, the extra errors the rule lets through at a stakes level are bounded in expectation by the budget times the ratio of the real to the expected share of that level, whatever the agent's error process, as long as the errors the check misses are not targeted. The rule also includes withdrawal at the first error, resets after a model change, recertification trials drawn from past errors, and hard caps that no evidence can lift.
2. **A measurement of the delegation gap on real agents.** For each model, environment and action type, we measure the confirmed error rate of the agent, with intervals at the task level, and its distance to the bar that the budget requires (Section 5, Q2). Whatever the verdict on the rule, this says how far current agents are from earning autonomy on each kind of action under an explicit budget.
3. **A preregistered protocol that can fail.** The plan, the rule, the code, the seeds and the decision labels were frozen and registered before any confirmatory episode existed (Section 4): the preregistration was registered on OSF on 10 November 2026 at 05:00 UTC, and wave 1 started at 05:19 UTC the same morning (an OSF registration can still be cancelled for 48 hours; it was not). Every automated alert is reviewed on blind sheets and can be lifted only for one of four pre-written reasons with proof; the author blind-reviews a sample in each wave. The calibration/confirmation split is drawn from a public randomness beacon only after the confirmed verdicts are frozen. Negative outcomes (rule refuted, safe but not useful, nothing to delegate, insufficient evidence, instrument insufficient) are labelled and published in all cases.

**Results in brief.** No configuration reached the pre-registered level of evidence required for delegation (label R4). Only Haiku on WorkBench had enough volume to be judged, and its reversible writes err 8.6 % of the time against a 0.76 % bar. Across the 40 observed action types, 20 have an error rate established above their bar and none can be shown below it at these volumes (Section 6). The safety criterion was not refuted, but with almost no power (Section 6.3); utility could not be judged.

The experiment is deliberately modest in scope: one provider, three models, four public environments, default settings, an offline replay with a simulated human validator. Section 8 lists what it cannot show. In particular, at the planned volumes the rule could not reach autonomy at medium or high stakes: the required runs (204 to 733 consecutive successes at medium stakes, 2,582 to 12,653 at high stakes) exceed what any configuration produces on one type. The experiment tests the rule's safety mainly at low stakes, and says so in advance.

## 2 Related work

**Quality control and continuous sampling.** Continuous sampling plans (CSP) were introduced by Dodge (1943) for products "manufactured by conveyor or other straight line continuous processes" (p. 264). CSP-1 alternates 100 % inspection until i conforming units in a row, and inspection of a fraction f afterwards; the first defect returns the process to 100 % inspection. Tolerant variants (CSP-2 and CSP-3; Dodge and Torrey, 1951, as reported by Hryniewicz, 2011) return to full inspection only if a further defect follows the first within a short window; our rule withdraws at the first error and the guarantee of Section 3.4 does not cover such variants. Multi-level plans (Lieberman and Solomon, 1955, p. 687) move up one level after i successive non-defective inspected units and down one level at a defect; tightened plans (Derman, Littauer and Solomon, 1957, p. 395) drop r levels at a defect (MLP-r × 1) or, in the MLP-T variant, revert straight to full inspection. The classical average outgoing quality limit (AOQL) assumes a stationary defect rate. Its unrestricted version (UAOQL; Lieberman, 1953; Derman, Johns and Lieberman, 1959) bounds outgoing quality without the assumption of statistical control: Lieberman proves AOQL = (k − 1)/(k + i) for a sampling fraction 1/k, that is (1 − f)/(1 + if), when each defective found is replaced, and shows that it is attained when the process produces only defectives during partial inspection and only good items during full inspection (pp. 480-482). Wald and Wolfowitz (1945) had already observed that "the Dodge SP does not guarantee the AOQL under all circumstances", Dodge's formulas being derived under statistical control (p. 47). Hryniewicz (2011) reports that the UAOQL is often criticised as describing hardly realistic conditions, and presents Hillier's (1964) jump model as more realistic: Hillier assumes the process jumps out of control and defines the average extra defectives limit (AEDL), the upper limit to the expected number of defectives that pass before the plan adjusts to the deterioration (pp. 161-163). Our formulas come from this overview and from Rodriguez and Jeske (2018). We read all these articles in the original except Dodge and Torrey (1951), whose content we report from Hryniewicz (2011). The bound we use (Section 3.4) is the cycle argument of the UAOQL, adapted to a plan in which, after promotion, inspection is a post-hoc automatic check with detection probability d and detected errors are not prevented. It gives at most 1/(1 + N d) executed errors per write, in expectation. We do not claim it as new. We have not found it stated in this form in the sources we read, which bound outgoing quality with the factor (1 − f) because inspected defects are removed (Lieberman, 1953; Derman, Johns and Lieberman, 1959).

**Levels of autonomy.** Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens (2000) proposed types and levels of human interaction with automation. Feng, McDonald and Zhang (2025) define levels of autonomy for AI agents, certified by a third party but without a statistical mechanism for moving between levels. Our three tiers ("ask before", "act then notify", "act alone") correspond loosely to inspection levels of multi-level plans. This correspondence is a design choice, not a sourced result. Industry write-ups describe graduated autonomy with promotion and demotion, without published tests (e.g. Arora, Kezhukoot and Prabakaran, 2026, who score a rolling window of 50 actions). Anthropic (2026) measures how users' auto-approval of agent actions grows with experience (roughly 20 % of sessions for new users, over 40 % with experience), which describes trust without guaranteeing anything. The Digital Apprentice (Weber and Taneja, 2026) proposes per-skill autonomy tiers that are passed only when empirical evidence justifies it (v1, Sec. 2): promotion requires, over windows of N consecutive outputs, a correction rate below a threshold and lower than three windows earlier, plus a recorded human authorization (Eq. 1-3), and demotion is automatic when the rate exceeds a demotion threshold; N and the thresholds are not derived from an error budget and no bound is stated. Practitioner blogs already propose "earned autonomy" with fixed thresholds (e.g. a write error rate below 0.5 % sustained over 500 operations or 30 days), and one of them already tracks the error rate by operation type rather than overall (Pan, 2026a); another transposes lot acceptance sampling to agent outputs, without a formula or an error budget (Pan, 2026b). Per-type tracking is therefore not new in itself. What we add is a run length N derived from an error budget set in advance, a guarantee on the extra errors, and a preregistered measurement.

**Error budgets and autonomy.** Site-reliability engineering uses error budgets. The CASE framework (Telukunta, Lilis and Baron, 2026, v1) extends the error budget to decision quality, B = (1 − SLO) × N_decisions over a decision population (p. 10), and makes autonomy a controlled variable, a(t) = g(B_remaining(t)) with g monotone non-decreasing: autonomous scope expands while the budget is healthy, contracts toward human review as it depletes, and halts expanding on exhaustion (Eq. 7, p. 10). It already states that "autonomy is earned against a measured error budget rather than granted by approval memo" (p. 11). The function g is left unspecified (p. 28). The authors derive no statistical evidence requirement, no run length and no bound on the errors that delegation lets through, and they report no test of the mechanism (their three studies validate structure, p. 23). Their five levels are a maturity model of the governance architecture, not levels of agent autonomy, although the upper levels presuppose budget-modulated autonomy (pp. 23-24). In both CASE and our rule a budget drives autonomy; the mechanism differs. CASE ties autonomy to the budget remaining, with no stated granularity (it differentiates by risk class, p. 14, but not by evidence per action type); we derive in advance, per action type, the run length N required for promotion, withdraw at the first error, and state a bound on the extra errors.

**Earned authority.** The closest work is Zhang and Zhang (2026), *Are You Still the Agent I Authorized? Earned Authority under a Fixed Ceiling for Evolving Agents*. It formalises, under its assumptions A1 to A5, an agent's authority that may decrease freely, grows only when an evidence relation holds, and never exceeds a ceiling fixed by the user; after a mutation of the agent (memory, tools, workflows), an envelope fixed at grant time decides whether the grant survives, and a grant outside the envelope is suspended (v1, p. 4). Our caps (Section 3.2) follow the idea of a ceiling that evidence cannot lift, which Zhang and Zhang state in this form, building on earlier work they cite (Progent; Saltzer and Schroeder). We see our study as complementary. Zhang and Zhang provide a formal framework; we specify a concrete instantiation of its (abstract) evidence relation (N verified consecutive successes per type, with N derived from a budget) and test it empirically on real agents. Their paper presents itself as a formalisation without empirical evaluation (we read v1 in full; page numbers refer to the PDF).

**How this work differs from Zhang and Zhang.** Zhang and Zhang (2026) formalise authorization continuity for evolving agents: a transition envelope decides whether a grant survives a mutation, an immutable effect ceiling bounds all authority, and active authority may fall freely but rise only when an evidence relation holds. That relation is left abstract, with no count, threshold or bound (Def. 5, p. 9), and the paper is a formalisation with no empirical evaluation; it outlines a replay study of mutations (grant persistence, effect leakage, false invalidations, interaction cost) without running it. We instantiate the evidence relation. Evidence is a run of N consecutive verified successes on one action type, withdrawn at the first error, with N derived in advance from an error budget per stakes level; under immediate detection this bounds the extra errors that delegation lets through. We test the rule in a preregistered replay of recorded episodes from three models in four public environments, and measure how far each model's confirmed error rate lies from the bar the budget requires. Their ceiling bounds effects against adversarial amplification, while evidence beneath it is a reliability mechanism (p. 9), not an adversarial bound (Remark 2, p. 11); our rule allocates authority by reliability beneath such a ceiling, and its bound is likewise a reliability bound, valid in expectation and void if misses are targeted.

**Learned or dynamic permission systems.** Dynamic permission systems adapt the policy to the task or the agent's current state rather than to its record (e.g. Progent, Shi, T. et al., 2025). Ou (2026) learns a human's allow/ask/block boundary from approve/deny feedback (reversibility is one input feature) and is evaluated only in simulation, against a synthetic oracle that holds the safety veto (irreversible, sensitive and low-trust actions). It learns the supervisor's preference rather than the agent's reliability. AI-control protocols (Greenblatt et al., 2023) combine an initial human audit with monitoring. Their threat model is adversarial and their metric a probability of safety, not an excess error rate. Safe policy improvement and Seldonian algorithms (Thomas et al., 2019), selective classification (El-Yaniv and Wiener, 2010; Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2017) and clinical non-inferiority practice share one principle that we adopt in the success criterion: a competitor's failure is never a win, and the inert reference must be beaten.

**Agent benchmarks.** τ-bench (Yao et al., 2024) and its successor τ²-bench / τ-bench 2.0 (Barres et al., 2025) simulate customer service with a simulated user, a domain policy, and grading by final database state and expected actions. We use version 2.0 (commit 033bf08), with the airline, retail and banking_knowledge domains. The banking domain is not part of τ²-bench (airline, retail, telecom): it comes from τ-Knowledge (Shi, Q. et al., 2026, arXiv:2603.04370), where it is called τ-Banking and has about 700 documents; the paper tests dense retrieval, BM25 and terminal search. WorkBench (Styles et al., 2024) provides 690 office tasks (e-mail, calendar, CRM, project management, analytics) with 26 tools. It grades the final state of the databases only, with no LLM judge ("outcome-centric evaluation"): unlike τ-bench's action comparison, it does not compare tool calls. SABER (Cuadron et al., 2025) studies state-modifying (mutating) steps on τ-bench and SWE-Bench Verified, and reports that many original τ-bench instructions lack sufficient detail (31 of 50 in airline, 53 of 115 in retail); the counts may differ in version 2.0, whose reserved tasks we use. The Holistic Agent Leaderboard (Kapoor et al., 2025) evaluates models, scaffolds and benchmarks jointly and shows that scaffold choice changes accuracy and cost, which supports treating the full configuration, not the model alone, as the unit on which our resets act. It also reports agents misusing credit cards in τ-bench airline tasks, relevant to money-type errors. AgentAbstain (Liu et al., 2026) evaluates whether agents know when not to act, including post-hoc abstention, where agents execute irreversible actions before recognising abstention triggers. None of these benchmarks provides per-action verdicts or a reversibility annotation; we derive both.

**Statistics of evaluations.** Miller (2024) recommends clustered standard errors when questions are drawn in related groups, and statistical inference on question-level paired differences when two models are compared. We follow both ideas: the independent unit is the task, all rules are compared on the same traces and the same random draws, and intervals come from a task-level jackknife-t chosen by a coverage simulation before the freeze. Simultaneous inference over cells uses the max-T procedure of Westfall and Young (1993). Proportions use Clopper–Pearson bounds (Clopper and Pearson, 1934) computed on an effective sample size that accounts for within-task correlation, estimated with a stratified version (our extension) of the Fleiss and Cuzick (1979) intra-class estimator for dichotomous judgments. The use of a public randomness beacon to draw an allocation after a sealed plan follows NIST's documented use case and a recent preprint (Panossian, 2026), whose seal is tied to one future pulse with no alternative; we add a written drand fallback.

## 3 The earned-autonomy rule

The rule is written in nine pieces (REGLE.md, frozen). We summarise the pieces that matter for the experiment. Where this summary and the frozen texts differ, the frozen texts prevail.

### 3.1 Unit of evidence: the action type and its stakes

Actions are classified by what they do, not by the tool that performs them, through a frozen table that maps each benchmark tool to a type, a stakes level and reversibility (`banc/table_outils.json`, v1). Each type has a single stakes level:

| Stakes | Types |
|---|---|
| Low | read (no effect); reversible write; internal message |
| Medium | durable write; execution and delegation |
| High | external output; money; guardrail or rights; illegible effect |

An action with two effects takes the higher stakes (cancelling an order also refunds it, so it counts as money). A tool absent from the table is treated as "illegible effect", at high stakes. Evidence stays within its type: successes on one type never count for another, and an error on one type does not freeze the others. Doubts raised by the agent (asking to transfer to a human) are neutral: they cost a validation but are neither a success nor an error.

Reads are autonomous from the start and enter neither the load nor the damage count; the instrument gives them no verdict. The budget is counted per *write*. This is a declared second correction of the rule, made after the pilot showed that reads were 85–89 % of actions in customer service. Counting per action made the bars absurd where writes are rare, and let an agent widen its own bar by reading more. Results are reported under both definitions (per write, primary; per action including reads, secondary).

### 3.2 Caps

Some actions are always validated, whatever the evidence: modifying a guardrail or the agent's own rights, attesting the human's consent, revealing a secret. Money above a per-environment amount is also capped (2,500 USD in airline, 500 USD in retail, 400 USD in banking, roughly the top quarter of expected amounts), as is a first message to an unknown recipient. Only the human changes a cap, by a dated decision. The idea of a ceiling that evidence cannot lift follows Zhang and Zhang (2026); the amounts and the first-message cap are ours.

### 3.3 Tiers, promotion and withdrawal

There are three tiers: (1) *ask before*: the write is validated before execution; (2) *act then notify*: it executes, and the same validator can correct it afterwards (reversible types only); (3) *act alone*. Irreversible types go directly from tier 1 to tier 3.

The main rule, **B-all** (named B-tout in the frozen plan), is applied per type, in the order of the sequence:

1. tier 3 is reached after N consecutive verified successes on the type;
2. any error, at any tier, resets the run counter to 0;
3. an error at tier 3 withdraws autonomy. At medium or high stakes the type returns to tier 1 and a *trial* opens; at low stakes it drops to tier 2 if reversible, tier 1 otherwise. Any medium- or high-stakes error opens a trial;
4. after a medium- or high-stakes error, the next climb requires ⌈1.25 N⌉ successes ("the error stays"); the lengthening does not accumulate;
5. the counter is frozen while a trial is open; a trial consists of three variants of the faulty case, and in the offline replay a variant is another recorded episode of the same task played by the current model. Trials are played "dry": they cause no damage and their validation load is counted separately. A failed trial imposes a wait of 50 writes of the type;
6. the counter is capped at the required length;
7. a model change (or extended rights) resets the counter. Remembered medium- and high-stakes errors become trials for the new configuration; low-stakes errors are dropped;
8. unverifiable writes and doubts are neutral: no success is ever assumed.

Below tier 3, tier 2 of reversible types follows a Wald (1945) sequential score with fixed starting settings. Its parameters are (p_good, p_bad) = (0.10, 0.30) at low stakes and (0.05, 0.20) at medium stakes, with α = β = 5 %.

An automated check judges each write; the agent never judges itself. An automated alert becomes an error only after confirmation (Section 4.3).

### 3.4 From an error budget to N, and the guarantee

**Budget.** Blind, before seeing whether the corrected rule passed the synthetic simulation, the author fixed the extra errors he would accept relative to "validate everything", in exchange for fewer validations: 0.5 per 100 writes at low stakes, 0.05 at medium and 0.005 at high. Each level is ten times stricter than the one below. The order of magnitude was anchored, qualitatively, on human error rates for routine tasks in human reliability analysis (HEART, Williams, 1985; THERP, Swain and Guttmann, 1983), whose data are old and debated. We state no range from these sources.

**Bar.** The budget B_ℓ of level ℓ is converted into a per-type error rate, the *bar*, for each environment e:

  bar(ℓ, e) = B_ℓ / (0.95 × 100 × q(ℓ, e)),

where 0.95 is the share of errors the simulated validator stops (it misses 5 %). q(ℓ, e) is the expected share of level-ℓ writes, computed from the expected actions of all tasks of the environment: the numerator excludes capped writes, the denominator includes all typed expected writes. A level with no expected write keeps a fallback bar from the synthetic exploration profile.

**Run length.** With d = 0.95 the assumed share of errors that the automated check detects, B-all uses

  N = ⌈1 / (d × bar)⌉ − 1,

computed in exact fractions. A secondary rule, B-regular, uses the smallest N such that $N^N/(N+1)^{N+1} \le d \times \mathrm{bar}$, which is enough for an agent with a constant error rate but not for arbitrary behaviour.

**Table 1. Bars and run lengths per environment** (frozen; bars rounded for reading; the configuration file holds exact fractions). Cell: bar (N for B-all; N for B-regular).

| Environment | Low stakes | Medium stakes | High stakes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Airline | 1/12 (12; 5) | 1/194 (204; 75) | 1/12,020 (12,653; 4,655) |
| Retail | 1/38 (39; 15) | 1/697, fallback (733; 270) | 1/11,335 (11,931; 4,389) |
| Banking | 1/86 (90; 33) | 1/637 (670; 247) | 1/2,453 (2,582; 950) |
| WorkBench | 1/132 (138; 51) | 1/580 (610; 225) | 1/4,698, fallback (4,945; 1,819) |

**Guarantee.** Consider one type at tier 3. Each autonomy period is preceded by at least N verified successes of that type and ends at the first detected error. Let d be the share of errors that the automated check detects after execution, independently of the agent's choices. Count in cycles: N successes, then errors until the first detected one, which takes 1/d errors on average, all executed. The expected number of errors executed at tier 3 per write of the type is then at most 1/(1 + N d), whatever the sequence of errors. This is the cycle argument of the unrestricted AOQL (1 − f)/(1 + if) of Lieberman (1953), with i = N and f read as d, without the factor (1 − f) in the numerator, because a detected error is not prevented: detection follows execution. If d = 1, the bound is deterministic: at most one executed error per N + 1 writes. If d < 1, it is a ratio of expectations per cycle, hence a long-run bound; on a finite sequence, the last, incomplete cycle is not covered by this statement. It falls if the errors the check misses are targeted. The preregistered N, drawn from N + 1 ≥ 1/(d × bar), is slightly conservative: since d ≤ 1, 1/(1 + N d) ≤ 1/(d (N + 1)) ≤ bar, so the simplification never weakens the guarantee. Summing over the types of a level, let s be the real share of non-capped writes of the level among the denominator writes, and q the expected share used for the bar. Two 0.95 factors must be kept apart: the validator's stop rate (1 − e, inside the bar) and the detection share d (inside N). With immediate and complete detection (d = 1 in the bound), the errors executed at tier 3 stay below budget × s/q per 100 writes, and, averaged over validator draws, the extra errors relative to "validate everything" stay below 0.95 × budget × s/q. This is the construction bound published for every cell (plan, 1.4). If only a share d = 0.95 of errors is detected, as assumed when N was computed, the expected executed errors stay below budget × s/(0.95 q) and the extra errors below budget × s/q: the budget still holds whenever s ≤ q, which is what the factor d in N buys.

**Assumptions.** The bound holds (i) for errors that the check detects, (ii) when detection is immediate, and (iii) relative to a validator that never rejects a correct write. If detection arrives k writes late, the bound becomes roughly (1 + k)/(N + 1) per write. An error that the check never sees is not covered: it counts as a success. N was computed with d = 0.95, and the bound holds in expectation over misses that are independent of the agent's choices; if misses are targeted (the agent errs exactly where the check is blind), no bound holds. The guarantee also does not cover tolerant variants, where a cycle may contain several detected errors. Finally, the bound is relative to the *expected* mix q. If the real share s of a level exceeds q, the bound grows by s/q. For banking at low stakes, the pilot mix gives s/q ≈ 2.2, so the construction bound there is about 2.1 times the budget.

**Choice of B-all.** B-all was chosen over B-regular by the author on 4 November 2026, before the freeze. A synthetic exploration (E1, exploratory and deposited) had selected B-regular by a pre-written choice rule: lower validation load (63.8 against 72.7 per 100 non-read actions). But E1's robustness alert fired. With 10 % of errors missed by the automated check, B-regular exceeded the budget in 9 cells (up to 1.05 times); with hard cases always missed, in 75 cells (up to 2.26 times). B-all's figures were 0 and 6 cells (up to 1.22 times). B-regular, and two Wald-based rules with p_bad = bar and p_good = bar/2 or bar/4 (A½, A¼), are replayed as secondary rules.

## 4 Experimental setup

### 4.1 Environments and tasks

We use four environments from two public benchmarks: τ-bench 2.0 (`airline`, `retail`, `banking_knowledge`; commit 033bf08) and WorkBench (commit 49c7dfd). Airline is graded with substring matching for communication and retail with forced state evaluation, so that no LLM judge is used. Banking uses BM25/grep retrieval without third-party calls. WorkBench uses all tools, structured outputs and logged traces, and has no simulated user. A development set of 20 % of the tasks of each environment (stratified by file for WorkBench) was drawn on 5 November 2026. It was used for a pilot and for engine tests only; no development task enters the verdict. The reserved tasks are 40 (airline), 91 (retail), 77 (banking) and 552 (WorkBench).

### 4.2 Models and settings

The agents are `claude-haiku-5-5`, `claude-sonnet-5-5` and `claude-opus-5-5`, called through the provider API with default settings. No temperature is set, since the API rejects it for these models. Default effort is medium for Haiku and Opus and high for Sonnet. The τ-bench simulated user is `claude-haiku-5-5` for all configurations. Prompt caching is enabled for τ-bench and unavailable for WorkBench's client. Each τ-bench episode is limited to 100 turns and 30 minutes. For each call, the launcher records the model name returned by the API and the tool definitions received by the agent; a change stops the run.

### 4.3 The instrument: per-action verdicts and alert review

**Automated check.** Every write made with a tool of the frozen table is judged by exact comparison with the task's expected actions (`banc/verdicts.py`), without any LLM judge. A write is *correct*, a *duplicate*, *refused* or *wrong*. A duplicate with no extra effect on an idempotent tool is neutral; a duplicate that changes the state is an error. A write absent from the expected list is an error. A refused write is an error for the agent's record but not damage. A small number of comparison rules were adjusted on the pilot before the freeze, all logged. For example, payment_id is compared only when a payment is actually recorded, and only the tool's name is compared when the agent gives a tool to the user. Task success rates, from each benchmark's final-state check, are reported alongside, because the check does not see work left undone.

**Alert confirmation.** An *alert* is a write marked wrong, duplicate or refused. Every alert is reviewed by fresh Claude instances on blind sheets: no model name, episode number, rank or task identifier, only an opaque alert ID. Blindness holds for the sheets and is enforced by instruction, not by a technical barrier on the reading environment. In wave 2, the 2,529 alerts were reviewed in 1,428 batches by fresh Claude Opus 5.5 instances run headless through Claude Code on a subscription (each batch in its own instance, with only the project settings); the reviewing model was not recorded for the 232 alerts of wave 1. Coverage is published per environment. An alert can be lifted only for one of four reasons written in advance, with proof: (a) defective task; (b) mere rephrasing of a free-text field; (c) another path allowed by the policy that produces the user's intended effect; (d) a defect in our comparison code. Any other alert is confirmed. A task is *defective*, and removed for all models and rules, only if its own files contradict each other: policy, initial data, simulated user's instruction, expected actions (four cases, a1–a4). An episode can signal a defect but never prove it. The author may oppose a removal before the verdict freeze; the task then stays and its alerts become confirmed errors.

**Blind human review.** In each wave, the author blind-reviews 15 alerts drawn at random (10 lifted, 5 confirmed, at most one per conversation), without the first reviewer's opinion. Agreement of at least 13 out of 15 keeps the review. Otherwise a second round of 15 is judged on both rounds (at least 26 out of 30). If that fails, the disagreement is persistent: no reason is rewritten after the freeze, and the label is computed twice, with retained decisions and with raw verdicts, keeping the less favourable one. If the first review lifted a real high-stakes error, all lifted high-stakes alerts are re-reviewed (plan 4.4).

**Audit of tasks without alerts.** After the last wave, five alert-free tasks per environment are read in full by blind Claude instances. Any write judged correct that the policy or the user's request contradicts is recorded as an "invisible error". This changes no verdict. It is published as a measure of what exact comparison misses.

### 4.4 Waves and spending caps

Episodes are interleaved by relative rank, environment and model in an order drawn with a recorded seed, so that a stop leaves balanced coverage. Wave 1 is about 10 % of each configuration (1,430 episodes, 60.53 USD estimated at pilot costs). It stops before any new episode at 80 % of its cap, which is 1.5 times the wave estimate. Wave 2's cap is 1.25 times an estimate recomputed on wave 1, within the remaining budget. It stops at the smaller of 95 % of the cap and the cap minus 15 USD. If 1.25 times the recomputed total exceeds the remaining budget, all pass counts are multiplied by the same factor, rounded down, with at least one pass per task and model. Technical failures are relaunched once, then excluded. Episodes stopped without a usable trace (WorkBench tool exception, context overflow, 35-minute kill) are excluded without relaunch and counted by configuration and reason. No episode is added in view of results. Between waves, only a pre-written list of checks is allowed (cost, run health, instrument coverage, alert review, defective tasks, returned model name). No rule replay, load, damage or error rate is computed before the final replay.

### 4.5 Calibration, confirmation and the public draw

The reserve is split by task, half and half, stratified by environment (and by file for WorkBench), within task pairs frozen before registration. The pairs are computed without randomness from expected actions, so that rare types are balanced. The *calibration* half measures each configuration's confirmed error rate by type (Q2), supplies the oracle's rates and decides which configurations are delegable; the rule is never replayed on it. The *confirmation* half is where the frozen rule is replayed, once, and where the verdict is read.

During collection the split does not exist, so no one can know which half a task belongs to. After the last wave, the confirmed verdicts and removed tasks are frozen, and their manifest is registered on OSF. The split seed is the SHA-256 of a fixed label, the manifest's hash and the first NIST Randomness Beacon pulse dated at least 72 hours after submission of that registration (an OSF registration can be withdrawn within 48 hours), with drand as a written fallback. Anyone can redo the draw from the public pulse and the registered hashes. The final replay then runs once, from one script, which refuses to start if the repository differs from the frozen manifest.

### 4.6 Offline replay

The rule and its comparators are replayed on the recorded episodes. For a configuration and a task set, episodes are shuffled, their write actions laid end to end, and reads and preparations removed. Every rule then sees the same sequence and the same validator draws (one uniform draw per write). At tier 1 an error passes if the draw falls below e = 5 %; at tier 2 it executes and is corrected unless the draw falls below e; at tier 3 every error is damage. *Extra errors* are errors executed at tier 3 that "validate everything" would have stopped, per 100 writes, per stakes level. The *validation load* counts tier-1 validations (capped writes included), tier-2 corrections and doubts, per 100 actions excluding reads and preparations. Point estimates are means over R0 = 2,000 replays without resampling. Model-change scenarios concatenate the confirmation sequence of model X with that of model Y. There are 6 ordered pairs per environment, each in two modes: *announced* (reset at the junction) or *silent* (no event, which also serves as the "no reset" comparator). This gives 48 sequences. All sub-seeds derive from one master seed drawn at the freeze.

## 5 Preregistered analysis

This section restates the registered analysis plan. It adds nothing to it.

### 5.1 Cells and comparators

A *cell* is a configuration or a model-change sequence at one stakes level: (12 + 48) × 3 = 180 cells. The mandatory comparators are: **validate everything** (every write at tier 1; the reference for extra errors); the **fixed list** (reads and reversible writes at tier 3, everything else at tier 1); and **risk-based triage** (all low-stakes types at tier 3, the rest at tier 1). Reported without effect on the label: approve everything; autonomy after three successes; random delegation at the same rate as the rule, type by type; an oracle that delegates a type if its median calibration rate is at most the bar; a retrospective oracle; and the secondary rules (B-regular, A½, A¼).

### 5.2 Criteria and labels

**Point 0, instrument validity.** Each wave ended with its blind review kept, or followed the persistent-disagreement rule. The coverage of the instrument (share of writes with a verdict) must be at least 0.95. Otherwise the label is R5, and everything is still computed and published.

**H1, safety (eliminatory).** B-all keeps the budget in every cell. A cell fails only if its excess is *established* by the max-T procedure (Section 5.3). Cells where B-all never reaches tier 3 in any of the R0 replays are "held, not put to the test" and leave the max-T family. A failure gives R1. The withdrawal criterion (at most one detected error before withdrawal at medium and high stakes) holds by construction under immediate detection. It is checked as a code control, not as a route to refutation; its delayed-detection version is reported.

**Delegable configuration.** A configuration is delegable if at least one earned type has a median calibration rate at most its bar. The median rate is the one-sided 50 % Clopper–Pearson bound on the effective size. A type is *judgeable* if zero errors would bring its median rate under its bar.

**No delegable configuration.** If no configuration is delegable, the label is R4 (preregistered name: "nothing to delegate: unreliability established"; Section 7 and limitation 21 state what it means here) only if (i) at least one type is judgeable, and (ii) every configuration with a judgeable type has at least one judgeable type whose one-sided 95 % Clopper–Pearson lower bound at ρ = 1 lies above its bar. Otherwise it is "insufficient evidence", with its reason: no judgeable type; excess not established; or excess established only under the configuration's own estimated correlation, not at ρ = 1.

**H2, utility.** With the budget kept, B-all must beat each mandatory comparator. "Validate everything" is beaten only on load: B-all's mean load over delegable configurations (equal weights) must be lower, with the paired-difference interval entirely and strictly below 0. The fixed list and the triage are beaten either by an established budget excess in at least one cell, or on load in the same way. Otherwise the label is R2. A rule that delegates nothing therefore cannot be confirmed: a competitor's fault is never a win.

**Final label**, in order of precedence: **R5** (instrument insufficient), **R1** (rule refuted), **insufficient evidence**, **R4** (preregistered name "nothing to delegate"; see Section 6.2), **R2** (safe but not useful), **confirmed** (H1 and H2 hold, with at least one delegable configuration).

**What R1 and "confirmed" can and cannot mean (plan 1.4).** With immediate detection, B-all's bound holds for any sequence: every error executed at tier 3 follows at least N successes of the same type, so at a given level the executed errors stay below budget × s/q per 100 writes, and the extra errors, averaged over validator draws, below 0.95 × budget × s/q. H1 can therefore hardly fail through the data. R1 can mean a mix gap (s above about 1.05 × q at a level where B-all reaches tier 3); it cannot mean, on its own, that the agents are unreliable, since B-all withdraws autonomy at the first error. The withdrawal criterion holds by construction and cannot refute the rule. "Confirmed" requires that B-all delegate in an established way with fewer validations than "validate everything", and that the risk triage be beaten by an established excess (where the triage keeps the budget, it necessarily asks for fewer validations than B-all); it cannot mean that B-all is safe at levels where it delegated nothing ("held, not put to the test"), nor beyond these benchmarks, models and settings. The part of the study that can really fail through the data is H2, together with the measured delegation gap.

**Q2, delegation gap (descriptive).** For each configuration and type, on the calibration half, we report: the error rate p = x/n (confirmed errors, refused included, over verifiable non-neutral writes); p/bar and upper bound/bar; the class (below the bar, above, indeterminate) at ρ = 1 and under the estimated ρ̂; the median rate; whether the type is judgeable; and the certification volume. Under R4 or insufficient evidence, the delegation gap becomes the main result.

**Random delegation (separate conclusion).** If B-all lets through fewer extra errors than random delegation at the same rate at one level at least and more at none, autonomy is called *earned*. If the difference is not shown, it is only *distributed*. If neither has extra errors, the comparison is *without object*. This comparison does not affect the label.

### 5.3 Intervals and multiplicity

The independent unit is the task. Intervals on load differences, on means over delegable configurations, on the comparison with random delegation and on proportions use a task-level jackknife-t (Student quantile with G − 1 degrees of freedom). It was selected before the freeze by a coverage simulation over ρ ∈ {0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9} and G ∈ {20, 40, 90} task clusters, with a worst coverage of 0.933 against a 0.93 threshold. That coverage was measured on paired load differences (Monte-Carlo error about 0.35 point); on the budget cells, an earlier simulation had given the jackknife-t a worst coverage of 0.549, which is why the safety test uses max-T (plan 5.13). Intervals on extra errors are therefore nominal 95 % intervals, read descriptively.

An *established excess* uses a Westfall–Young max-T over the family of testable cells. It is fed by a stratified task-cluster bootstrap with B = 2,000 replicates, each averaged over K = 10 orders and validator draws. The statistic T*_c is centred on the bootstrap mean and standardised by the cell's bootstrap SD. The critical value c* is the 97.5th percentile of the maximum over testable cells, floored at 0 (formula F3). An excess is established in cell c if estimate_c − c* × SD_c exceeds the budget. A cell is not testable if it has fewer than two carrying tasks, an undefined estimate, an undefined bootstrap SD, or a zero SD (within four float32 epsilons). Non-testable cells establish nothing and are published with their reason.

The nominal family-wise level is 5 %, one-sided. The level measured before the freeze is 4.7 % cumulated (28/600, Wilson 3.2–6.7 %). It was measured in a synthetic world with all testable cells at their budget boundary, the least favourable configuration measured, not the worst possible world. Its interval exceeds 5 %, so the measurement does not show that F3 holds 5 %. It was measured with B = 200, not 2,000, and is not measured for the comparators. Tested alone, the worst low-stakes WorkBench cell is falsely declared in excess in 27 of 200 replicates. If the family has one or two testable cells, the max-T is almost an uncorrected test, and the output says so. The nominal risk of declaring the fixed list beaten wrongly can reach 7.5 % (5 % max-T plus 2.5 % load interval). The choice of F3 is itself a declared deviation. The pre-set formula-choice rule, applied to the measurement draws only, designated F1, which over-rejected when all draws of the same quantity were counted (6.7 %, 40/600); the rule was then applied to all draws and retained F3.

A cell is "demonstrated" if its one-sided 95 % Clopper–Pearson upper bound on the effective sample size at ρ = 1 is at most the budget. At the planned volumes we expect few or no such cells. At zero damage, demonstrating 0.5 per 100 writes needs at least 598 independent writes, and errors cluster within tasks. The experiment can therefore refute the budget for B-all, but cannot demonstrate it configuration by configuration. Where no excess of B-all is established, its compliance rests on the construction bound and on the absence of an established excess.

### 5.4 Secondary and stress analyses (no effect on the label)

These analyses are: safety on the whole reserve; role swap (two-fold cross-validation); the secondary rules; the pre-freeze choice rule reapplied on the confirmation half; and raw verdicts (a verdict that changes between raw and confirmed is marked *fragile*). Stress tests: 10 % of confirmed errors unseen (miss10); evidence delayed by 10 writes (delay10); all errors of 10 % of tasks unseen; and validator miss rates e = 0, 2 and 10 %. We also report the budget per action (reads included), load with trials, and sensitivity to episode order. If plan text and frozen code diverge, the frozen code performs the confirmatory computation, and the text-based computation is published as exploratory.

### 6.3 Safety (H1)

**What "held" means here.** Point 1 is held in the sense of "not refuted", with almost no power: no established excess; the test could establish almost nothing (c* = 7.0, so an estimate would have had to lie more than seven standard errors above the budget); no cell demonstrated. H1 can hardly fail through the data. In the replay, every confirmed error at tier 3 is noticed at once, so the bound is deterministic (at most one executed error per N + 1 writes of the type), and the budget can only be exceeded if the actual share s of the type's writes exceeds the expected share q by about 5 % (plan 1.4). That condition (s/q above 1/0.95) holds for the point estimate in 24 of the 51 judged cells, on WorkBench and banking. Of the two hypotheses, the one the data could really refute is H2, measured by the delegation gap (Section 6.4).

### 6.4 Delegation gap (Q2)

The delegation gap compares, per configuration and action type, the confirmed error rate on the calibration half with the type's bar. Classes are computed at ρ = 1, conservative under an exchangeable within-task correlation and under the effective-size approximation (non-integer effective size and a beta law); a simulation run on the engine before any data found that this rule returns R4 wrongly at most 2.8 % of the time at the bar (`REGLE.md`, amendment of 7 November). No multiplicity correction is made over the 40 types. At these volumes the table can only return "above the bar" or "undetermined": the plan stated in advance (5.13) that no type could be proven below its bar, so the count "20 above, 0 below" is partly built in. Only one type is judgeable (judgeability uses n_eff under ρ̂, Section 6.2), and only that type bears on the label; the other classes are descriptive. Full table: Table 6, with the result tables; Figure 2 (`article/figures/fig2_gap.pdf`) shows rate/bar with the ρ = 1 interval on a log scale; zero rates are drawn hollow at 0.001, and types with fewer than 5 writes (banking/Haiku ILL, 2 writes; banking/Opus GAR, 3) are marked.

**Overview.** 40 types have data. 20 are above their bar at ρ = 1, 20 undetermined, none below. Of the 20 undetermined types, 13 have no observed error: on WorkBench, Opus and Sonnet internal messages (MSG, 0 of 58 each) and medium-stakes writes (DUR, 0 of 106 each); on airline, reversible writes (ECR) for Haiku (0 of 67), Opus and Sonnet (0 of 20 each), and medium-stakes writes for Opus and Sonnet (0 of 20 each); on banking, irreversible high-stakes writes (GAR) for all three models (0 of 10, 3 and 6); on retail, Sonnet's reversible writes (0 of 122). Absence of observed errors on so few writes says nothing in favour of delegation.

Rates and their ρ = 1 bounds come first below, then the ratios, level by level. The ratio "lower bound ÷ bar" is the proven quantity; ratios of the rate itself to the bar mostly reflect how low the bar is.

**Low stakes, outside banking.** On WorkBench, reversible writes have an error rate established above the 0.76 % bar for all three models: Haiku 8.6 % [4.1 ; 15.6] (two one-sided 95 % bounds, a 90 % interval; 914 writes; lower bound ÷ bar 5.5; the judgeable type), Sonnet 6.1 % [2.4 ; 12.5] (181 writes; 3.1) and Opus 5.5 % [2.0 ; 11.7] (182 writes; 2.7). Low-stakes types on airline and retail are undetermined: retail reversible writes run at 3.2 % (Haiku, 632 writes) and 3.8 % (Opus, 132) against a 2.6 % bar, with lower bounds at zero.

**High stakes, outside banking.** On airline, money-moving writes (ARG) have a rate established above the 0.0083 % bar for the three models: Haiku 13.3 % [0.6 ; 50.7] (308 writes; lower bound ÷ bar 70), Sonnet 11.1 % [0.3 ; 47.3] (72; 39) and Opus 8.5 % [0.1 ; 44.6] (71; 10). For Opus the excess is thin: 6 errors in 9 carrying tasks, lower bound 0.09 %. On retail, the same type is established above its 0.0088 % bar for Haiku 9.2 % [2.2 ; 23.6] (2,511 writes; 248), Sonnet 6.7 % [1.0 ; 20.5] (496; 118) and Opus 6.5 % [1.0 ; 20.5] (492; 109). At these bars a single error in 500 writes already gives a rate/bar ratio of about 20, and certifying the type would take 33,956 to 36,009 consecutive verified successes (plan 1.4): the high-stakes ratios are a fact about the budget chosen as much as about the agents. The rates themselves are the informative quantity, but they come from the calibration half: on the confirmation half the same types err 1.2 % to 7.7 % (3.8 % to 8.0 % on the whole reserve), the two halves differing by up to a factor of five, and the intervals above are wide.

**Banking, apart.** Banking rates are high: established above the bar for money-moving writes at 53.5 % (Haiku, 202 writes), 47.5 % (Opus, 80) and 56.0 % (Sonnet, 75); for medium-stakes writes at 44.9 %, 28.5 % and 41.4 %; for internal messages at 27.7 % (Haiku), 8.1 % (Opus) and 11.3 % (Sonnet); and for Sonnet's reversible writes at 32.6 %. They are consistent with task success of 18 % to 51 % on this benchmark, but banking is also where attribution to the agent is weakest: coverage 0.925, 19 real tools missing from the tool table, a knowledge base not shown to reviewers (so defective tasks could not be lifted), high stakes carried by the simulated client, and 89 of the 91 excluded episodes. These rates therefore do not separate agent error from setup error. Two banking excesses are marginal: Opus internal messages are established by 0.3 % (lower bound 1.165 % against a 1.161 % bar), and Haiku's ILL type rests on 2 errors in 2 writes.

**Correlation.** The estimated within-task correlation ρ̂ (stratified Fleiss–Cuzick) is 0.819 for WorkBench/Haiku, 1.0 for WorkBench Opus and Sonnet, 0.40 on airline, 0.40 to 0.47 on banking and 0.40 to 0.43 on retail; it is the larger of a floor (0.3) and the value pooled over all configurations, unless a configuration with at least 10 errors in at least 5 tasks has a larger value of its own (plan 5.13, N3). Every type established at ρ = 1 stays established under ρ̂. One type changes class under ρ̂: banking/Opus reversible writes (16.2 %, 74 writes) would be established above its bar for any ρ ≤ 0.924, against ρ̂ = 0.431 (21 types above under ρ̂).

**Delegable configurations.** None. The only configuration with a judgeable type, WorkBench/Haiku, has that type established above its bar; the label is R4 (Section 6.2).

**Whole reserve (descriptive, not a label).** The preregistered label is computed on the calibration half; pooling both halves can only be descriptive (Table 6 bis: 22 of 41 types above, none below). With roughly twice the volume, three configurations would have had a delegable type, all with zero observed errors: internal messages for Opus and Sonnet on WorkBench (0 errors in 120 writes each) and reversible writes for Sonnet on retail (0 in 258). Elsewhere the pooled rates can differ from the calibration rates by a factor of one to five; for instance, Haiku's reversible writes on retail show 31 errors in 1,286 writes (2.4 %, below a 2.6 % bar but not provably so). This pooled view shows where more volume could have changed the picture; it does not change the label, which the preregistration ties to the calibration half.

**Exploratory, dated 17 November 2026 (not preregistered; Appendix D).** Counting the 91 excluded episodes as errors adds 24 errors on the calibration half, all on banking, and one more established type (21 instead of 20), resting on 5 writes (Table X1). Allowing a share of the confirmed errors to be false alerts, the judgeable WorkBench/Haiku type stays above its bar with up to 63 % false confirmations, while banking/Opus internal messages would not withstand a single one (Table X2).

**Exploratory, dated 18 November 2026 (not preregistered; Appendix D, Tables X7 and X8).** A second exploratory pass read every confirmed plot error (`analytics.create_plot`) of the judged type: 56 of its 79 errors are plots, and 48 of them differ from the expected action only by the end date (the agent plots up to 30 November, "today", where the benchmark expects 29 November). Across the study, the reviewing instances decided that end-date case both ways (177 such plot alerts lifted, 144 confirmed, all models and both halves). Lifting it throughout leaves 31 errors (3.4 %, lower bound 0.86 %), still established, but by two errors (29 are needed); lifting also four plots whose window is shifted by one day would leave 27, not established, and the label would become "insufficient evidence". The label R4 therefore depends on how one recurring, arguably acceptable discrepancy is judged. Task success and action correctness also disagree: on WorkBench, 64 % to 82 % of episodes with a confirmed action error are successful tasks, and in every environment 47 % to 77 % of failed episodes have no confirmed action error (Table X7, exploratory). Failed episodes without a confirmed action error fail through reads, omissions or answers, which the action check does not count; on WorkBench, most errors inside successful tasks are the plot end-date case above, so this gap partly reflects the expected-path convention.

### 6.5 Utility (H2)

**Point 2 is not judged.** Under R4 the plan does not judge point 2 (5.14, Q15): the comparison with the witnesses is defined as an equal-weight mean over delegable configurations, and there are none. For the same reason the reduction of validations against "validate everything" as a share of the oracle's gain, and the paired differences, are not computable. "Validate everything" is not beaten. (The detail of the "point 2: success" line in `decision.csv`, "the three witnesses beaten", is the wording of the criterion written into the engine's output, not a result.) Figure 3 (`article/figures/fig3_load.pdf`) is therefore descriptive: per configuration, validation load against extra errors at low stakes for B-all and the three comparators, with no mean over delegable configurations; Table 7 gives the loads with intervals.

**What the rule did (Table 7, per 100 non-read actions).** B-all asked for 65.5 validations with Haiku on WorkBench, 54.9 with Sonnet and 55.1 with Opus; 84 to 86 on retail; 90 to 94 on banking; 98 to 100 on airline. The reduction on WorkBench comes almost entirely from tier 2 on reversible writes (63 %, 81 % and 81 % of those writes at tier 2): at tier 2 a write is executed, then corrected if the validator sees it, and it never counts as an extra error (plan 5.8). This is consistent with the definitions but must be read with care: tier 2 was granted on a type whose error rate is established above its bar. By an exploratory approximation, about 4.6 to 6.2 errors per 100 such writes were executed and then flagged on WorkBench (Appendix D, Table X5). On retail the reduction comes from tier 3 on reversible writes, granted with 1 to 5 promotions per replay.

**The witnesses' excesses, as an illustration of the gap.** The fixed list has an established excess (max-T) in one configuration, Haiku on WorkBench (5.5 extra errors per 100 writes, 11 times the budget); risk triage in two, Haiku on WorkBench (5.8, 12 times) and Haiku on banking (10.2, 20 times), all at low stakes. The 9 and 18 cells counted in Table 2 add model-change scenarios that replay the same episodes; they are repetitions, not new evidence. Sonnet and Opus on WorkBench sit at 6.6 and 6.1 times the budget under both witnesses without an established excess (c* = 3.77). The witnesses' excesses are true by construction once the delegation gap is known: B-all almost never grants tier 3 to WorkBench reversible writes at 5.5 % to 8.6 % errors (at most 0.2 % of replays), because it requires N consecutive verified successes of the type (Appendix B), whereas a list without error feedback delegates them. This does not hold for every type above its bar: banking/Opus internal messages reached tier 3 in 4.3 % of replays (Section 6.6, guarantee measure). The witnesses' excesses are therefore not a success of B-all. Where both kinds of rule delegate (retail reversible writes), nothing separates them: extra errors 0.31, 0 and 0.70 per 100 for the witnesses against 0.13, 0 and 0.14 for B-all, none established, with slightly fewer validations for the witnesses (83.4, 82.5 and 81.9 against 84.4, 84.1 and 85.8). The witnesses have no tier 2; a fixed list putting reversible writes at tier 2 would, under the same accounting, have no extra errors and fewer validations than B-all (Section 8).

### 6.6 Model changes

**Withdrawal criterion and scenarios (Table 8).** Across the 48 model-change scenarios (announced and silent), the withdrawal criterion of decision 5 passed in every scenario × type row, in the main analysis and in the delay10 version. At these levels (medium and high stakes, the scope of decision 5) the pass is empty: no tier-3 period followed any junction (`derive.csv`). At low stakes, autonomy did cross junctions (`retrait.csv`). On retail, Sonnet's reversible writes were at tier 3 at the junction in all 2,000 replays. When Sonnet was silently replaced by Haiku or Opus (3.2 % and 3.8 % errors against a 2.6 % bar; "became bad" in the secondary reading only, point estimate above the bar), withdrawal took a median of 61 and 40 writes of the type (95th percentile 226 and 108), with one error let through on average, and autonomy was granted again afterwards in 85 % to 100 % of replays. With an announced change, the reset withdrew autonomy at once. In the primary reading (interval entirely above the bar), no type that became bad held autonomy. The scenario cells of Table 5 (144 of the 180) were judged only at low stakes: 10 on WorkBench, 12 on banking, 10 on airline and 12 on retail; the 22 on airline and retail are "fragile" (fewer than 30 carrying tasks); none has an established excess.

**Guarantee measure (no threshold; plan 5.12).** Types classed above their bar on the calibration half still reached tier 3 in some replays (`garantie.csv`): banking/Opus internal messages (8.1 % errors) in 4.3 % of replays as a constant configuration and in up to 11.8 % after a silent change from Sonnet; banking/Sonnet internal messages in 8.3 % of replays after a silent change from Opus; banking/Haiku internal messages in at most 0.4 %; WorkBench reversible writes in at most 0.2 %.

**Trials.** Trials were opened most often on banking and retail with Haiku (168 and 106 per replay on average) and rarely passed: on banking, 0.2 to 0.4 passed per replay for 3.0 to 9.4 failed; on retail, 2.0 to 3.8 passed for 4.8 to 39.0 failed; on airline, 0.5 to 3.6 passed for 2.1 to 9.2 failed. ## 7 Discussion

**What R4 means, and what it does not.** On the calibration half, none of the twelve configurations has an action type whose median confirmed error rate lies under its bar; the same holds in the role swap and under raw verdicts (Table 2). In the only configuration with enough volume to be judged, Haiku's reversible writes on WorkBench err 8.6 % of the time against a 0.76 % bar, with a task-level lower bound of 4.1 % (Section 6.2). That is what R4 says. It does not say that the agents are unreliable in general: it says nothing about the eleven other configurations, 13 types show no observed error, and the judgeability that carries the label is narrow (limitation 21). It does not rank the three models either, since only Haiku's volume made a type judgeable (limitation 31). Nor does it mean that nothing could be delegated: on the whole reserve three configurations would have a delegable type, all with zero observed errors (Table 6 bis), and B-all did delegate during the replay (Section 6.5).

**What the delegation gap says about agents, and about the budget.** Descriptively, every one of the twelve configurations has at least one type whose lower bound at ρ = 1 lies above its bar (Table 6; not a label claim, mostly on non-judgeable types). Two kinds of facts must be kept apart. The error rates are facts about the agents under this instrument: 5.5 % to 8.6 % on WorkBench reversible writes, 6.5 % to 13.3 % on money-moving writes in airline and retail (Section 6.4). The ratios to the bar are, at high stakes, mostly facts about the budget chosen: a bar of about 0.008 % makes a single error in 500 writes already twenty times too many (limitation 11). On retail the high-stakes excesses stay established with up to 70 % to 79 % false confirmations (Table X2, exploratory); on airline only up to 31 % to 55 %, and the judgeable type up to 63 %. The author's blind sample (3 of 10 confirmed alerts lifted, five per wave; exact 95 % interval 7 % to 65 %) cannot rule such shares out: at its upper end, 9 of the 20 established excesses, including the airline ones and the one that carries R4, would be lost (limitation 23). On banking the rates are higher still, but there they do not separate agent error from setup error (limitation 27).

**Which constraint bound.** Volume. The mix did not limit any result, but it weakened the guarantee in part. The mix ratio s/q stayed between 0.72 and 1.14 in the 51 judged cells (Table 5). In 24 of them (11 on WorkBench, 13 on banking) the point estimate exceeds 1/0.95, so the construction bound there is up to 1.08 times the budget: the bound alone does not guarantee the budget in these cells, and compliance there rests only on the absence of an established excess. No cell carried the "guarantee outside the assumption" mention, which requires the lower bound of s/q to exceed 1/0.95. The pilot's banking ratio of 2.2 did not recur: s/q is 1.04 to 1.14 in banking's low-stakes cells, 14 of 15 of which were judged, with tier 3 reached in 0.2 % to 12 % of replays only. What bound the study most was volume: point 1 could establish almost nothing (c* = 7.0), 108 cells were never put to the test, and only one type was judgeable (limitations 11 and 22).

**Comparison with Zhang and Zhang (2026) and with CASE.** Zhang and Zhang formalise authority that falls freely, rises only under an evidence relation, and stays below a user-fixed ceiling; the relation is left abstract, with no count, threshold or bound (Def. 5, p. 9), and evidence beneath the ceiling is explicitly a reliability mechanism, not an adversarial bound (p. 9, Remark 2 p. 11). Our rule is one concrete instance of that relation, and our results show what such an instance costs: at the budgets we fixed, the evidence it demands (N = 12 to 138 consecutive verified successes at low stakes, 204 to 733 at medium stakes and 2,582 to 12,653 at high stakes; Section 3.4) is out of reach at medium and high stakes for one configuration of a current benchmark, and at low stakes it is out of reach for agents that err several percent of the time, since a run of N successes is then rarely completed. CASE ties autonomy to the remaining error budget through an unspecified non-decreasing function (Eq. 7, p. 10) and already speaks of autonomy "earned against a measured error budget" (p. 11). Our N is a different way to tie autonomy to a budget: sized in advance, per action type, with a stated bound, rather than a function of the budget remaining. Our data say nothing about CASE's mechanism, whose function g and service-level objective are unspecified.

**Implications for practice.** On these benchmarks and settings, an operator applying an error budget of this size could not have delegated any action type with evidence: 13 types had no observed error but too few writes, 7 had errors without an established excess, and 20 had established excesses, only one of them on a type with enough volume to be judged. Earning autonomy under such budgets takes far more verified actions than these benchmarks provide per configuration at our volumes: tens to hundreds of verified successes at low stakes and thousands to tens of thousands at high stakes (certification volumes of 34 to 394 and of 7,348 to 36,009 in Table 6). Two practical readings follow. A tier where actions are executed then flagged (tier 2) lowers validation load without delegating outright, but it lets through executed errors, about 4.6 to 6.2 per 100 such writes on WorkBench (Table X5, exploratory; limitation 26). And the budget itself is a decision: the gap shrinks or grows with the bar, and a team should set it knowing which volumes it implies before any run, as we did.

## 8 Limitations

Each limitation is marked *preregistered* (plan 1.4 and section 6), *added after the freeze* (before the results), or *added after the results*. Items 21 to 31 come from the results and from the audit of the results (17 November 2026).

1. **Offline trace evaluation.** *(Preregistered.)* A blocked or corrected action does not change the recorded episode: the episode continues as if the write had been executed. The agent does not know it is monitored. Rule comparisons are paired evaluations on identical traces, not counterfactual replays. They do not identify the effect of a rule in deployment, where interventions would change agent behaviour.
2. **Simulated validator.** *(Preregistered.)* It misses 5 % of errors and never rejects a correct write.
3. **Immediate detection.** *(Preregistered.)* Confirmed errors are applied to the action itself, although confirmation comes later by review. The delay10 stress test gives an idea of delayed detection.
4. **Detection share.** *(Preregistered.)* All budget conclusions are conditional on a detected share d = 0.95. An error invisible to exact comparison counts as correct; the alert-free audit and the miss10 and grouped-miss tests measure the sensitivity.
5. **Omissions are not errors.** *(Preregistered.)* Only writes made are judged. Task success rates are reported alongside.
6. **One expected path per task.** *(Preregistered.)* Correct actions may be flagged. Alerts that cannot be lifted with the evidence on the sheet stay errors: the banking knowledge base is not on the sheet, and WorkBench has undocumented data and no policy. This weighs against agents and may help B-all beat the comparators. Underspecified τ-bench instructions have been reported (Cuadron et al., 2025).
7. **Unchecked replies.** *(Preregistered.)* The agent's replies to the user in the conversation are not controlled by the rule. Errors induced by the simulated user are not separated from agent errors.
8. **Simulated user drift.** *(Preregistered.)* The simulated user is Haiku 5.5, the same for all agents and from the same family. It may contradict itself or drift within a conversation. In banking, pilot agents executed no banking operation themselves; they gave tools to the user. High stakes there are therefore carried by the simulated user.
9. **Same-provider reviewers.** *(Preregistered.)* Alert reviewers are Claude instances, from the same provider as the agents evaluated. The author's blind review covers 15 (or 30) cases per wave. *Strengthened after the results:* in wave 2 the reviewers were Claude Opus 5.5 instances reviewing episodes played in part by Opus 5.5; blindness rested on the instruction, not on a technical barrier (Deviations, D7); and the reviewing model of wave 1 was not recorded. Wave-2 batches closed in about 32 s each on average, which we could not explain; whether every sheet was read in full is not established. Of the author's four disagreements, three lifted a confirmed alert and one confirmed a lifted alert; 28 % of all alerts were lifted, so wrongly lifted alerts, which favour delegation, cannot be excluded either.
10. **Exclusions that favour delegation.** *(Preregistered.)* WorkBench tool exceptions mostly come from agent errors. Excluding these episodes probably removes errors and biases WorkBench error rates downward, in the direction that favours delegation. *Added after the freeze (Deviations, D2):* the same holds for banking. Technical failures there come from an empty agent message that τ-bench classes as `infrastructure_error`. They are relaunched once, then excluded. *After the results:* 91 episodes were excluded this way, 89 of them on banking (77 Haiku); counted as errors, they would add one established type (Appendix D, Table X1).
11. **Insufficient volume at high stakes.** *(Preregistered.)* No certification below the bar is possible at high stakes: 7,348 to 36,009 error-free successes would be needed. At planned volumes B-all is not expected to reach tier 3 at medium or high stakes. The rule's safety is therefore tested mainly at low stakes. Established excesses are detectable only when large and on types with many independent writes. *After the results:* at high stakes the rate/bar ratios mostly measure the height of the bars (one error in 500 writes already gives a ratio of about 20); they are a fact about the budget chosen, not a measure of agent reliability. Nothing is claimed for B-all at medium or high stakes, for other models or benchmarks, or for a real deployment.
12. **Mix mismatch.** *(Preregistered.)* Bars come from expected writes; the budget is measured on real writes. In banking at low stakes, s/q ≈ 2.2 with the pilot mix, so the construction bound is about 2.1 times the budget there. Compliance rests on the absence of an established excess. *After the results:* in the replay s/q was 0.72 to 1.14 in the judged cells; in 24 of 51 (WorkBench and banking) the construction bound exceeds the budget, up to 1.08 times.
13. **Repeated tasks.** *(Preregistered.)* Haiku replays the same tasks 50 times in airline and retail, so the rule may learn tasks rather than agents. The task-level bootstrap accounts for this in intervals, not in scope.
14. **Small samples.** *(Preregistered.)* Sonnet and Opus play one pass in WorkBench and three in banking. Airline has 20 tasks per half, a range where clustered inference is unreliable. Rare types (guardrail, external output, execution) are almost absent.
15. **Silent provider changes.** *(Preregistered.)* A behavioural drift of a served model without a change of its returned name would not be detected.
16. **Untested parts of the rule.** *(Preregistered.)* No injected faults (a declared deviation from the rule text); no modified instructions and no extended rights. Trials use other episodes of the same task, not true variants. Two comparators of the original rule text ("no stakes weighting", "no free doubt") are not replayed.
17. **Multiple-testing level.** *(Preregistered.)* The max-T level is measured at 4.7 % with an interval reaching 6.7 %, at B = 200 rather than 2,000, and not for the comparators (Section 5.3).
18. **Scope.** *(Preregistered.)* One provider, three models, four public environments, default settings.
19. **Prior knowledge.** *(Preregistered.)* Task specifications of all tasks, reserved ones included, were read before registration. Bars and task pairs were computed from the expected actions of all tasks. No agent episode on reserved tasks existed at registration. A separate exploratory strand of the project analysed logs of an operational agent; it is not used here.
20. **WorkBench grading is outcome-centric.** *(Added after the freeze, before the results.)* WorkBench scores the final state of its databases, whatever the action path (Styles et al., 2024), whereas our instrument compares each write to the expected actions. Its per-action verdicts are therefore derived by us, not provided by the benchmark, and a write may be flagged although the final state would have been accepted. Task success rates, from the final-state check, are reported alongside.
21. **R4 rests on one configuration, by a thin margin.** *(Added after the results.)* Only WorkBench/Haiku has a judgeable type, at n_eff × bar = 0.740 against a threshold of 0.6905; above ρ̂ = 0.885 the label would become "insufficient evidence" (approximate bootstrap interval of ρ̂ 0.660 to 0.924, exploratory, with a simpler estimator than the engine's). R4 says nothing about the eleven other configurations, and 13 types show no observed error. It does not establish that the agents are unreliable in general, nor that there is "nothing to delegate": on the whole reserve three configurations would have a delegable type, and B-all did delegate (tier 3 on retail and airline, tier 2 on WorkBench and banking, and tier 3 there too in some replays).
22. **Point 1 had almost no power.** *(Added after the results.)* B-all was not refuted, but an established excess required an estimate more than seven standard errors above the budget (c* = 7.0); no cell is demonstrated below budget, and the judged cells rest on 1 to 10 carrying tasks on airline and retail. "Not refuted" must not be read as "keeps the budget".
23. **False confirmations.** *(Added after the results.)* In the author's blind review, 3 of the 10 confirmed alerts reviewed were lifted. If such a share held overall, part of the confirmed errors would be false alerts. The judgeable type stays above its bar with up to 63 % false confirmations, just under the upper end of the blind sample's 95 % interval (65 %), at which 9 of the 20 established excesses would be lost; banking/Opus internal messages would not withstand one (Appendix D, Table X2). Confirmed errors are also not all agent errors: a drifting simulated client is not covered by the lift reasons (limitation 8; Deviations, D4).
24. **Confirmed errors in successful tasks.** *(Added after the results.)* An action error is not a failed task: on WorkBench, which scores the final state only, 64 % to 82 % of the episodes with a confirmed error are successful tasks (Appendix D, Table X3). Confirmed errors are not damage.
25. **Refused writes are counted.** *(Added after the results; the rule was preregistered.)* The frozen rule counts writes refused by the tool as errors without damage: 104 of the 1,067 confirmed errors of the calibration half (9.7 %; Appendix D, Table X4).
26. **Tier-2 accounting favours B-all over the witnesses.** *(Added after the results.)* A tier-2 write is executed, then corrected if the validator sees it, and never counts as an extra error; the witnesses have no tier 2. B-all's lower load on WorkBench comes from tier 2 granted to a type whose rate is established above its bar (about 4.6 to 6.2 errors per 100 such writes executed then flagged, exploratory). The witnesses' established excesses follow from the delegation gap and from this definition; they are not a success of B-all, and its utility is not judged under R4 ("validate everything" is not beaten).
27. **Banking.** *(Added after the results.)* Banking is where attribution to the agent is weakest: coverage 0.925; 19 real tools missing from the tool table; a knowledge base not shown to reviewers, so defective tasks could not be lifted; high stakes carried by the simulated client; 89 of the 91 exclusions. Its rates do not separate agent error from setup error.
28. **Audit of alert-free tasks.** *(Added after the results.)* 0 invisible errors in 21 writes leaves an upper bound near 13 %: the audit is weak evidence about what exact comparison misses.
29. **Barely tested withdrawal.** *(Added after the results.)* At medium and high stakes, no autonomy period followed any model change, so the decision-5 criterion passed without being tested. At low stakes, withdrawal after a silent change was observed on one type only (retail reversible writes, secondary reading: median 40 to 61 writes, one error let through on average; Section 6.6).
30. **The three secondary analyses share one threshold.** *(Added after the results.)* Raw verdicts, role swap and the main analysis give R4, but under raw verdicts WorkBench/Opus reversible writes become judgeable at 0.6930 against 0.6905, a margin of 0.4 %: they are not three independent confirmations.
31. **Dimensioning decides who can be judged.** *(Added after the results.)* Haiku played five times more episodes than Sonnet and Opus on WorkBench, airline and retail (on WorkBench, 1,380 against 276 per half) and about three times more on banking. Only Haiku had a judgeable type because of its volume, not because of its errors. The study cannot rank the three models, and does not.
32. **Inconsistent review of a recurring plot discrepancy, on which the label depends.** *(Added after the results, exploratory, 18 November 2026.)* On WorkBench, 56 of the 79 errors of the judged type are plots, 48 of which differ from the expected action only by an end date one day after the benchmark's; across the study the reviewing instances lifted that case 177 times and confirmed it 144 times. Lifting it throughout leaves 31 errors, established by two; lifting also four one-day window shifts leaves 27, not established, and the label would become "insufficient evidence" (Table X8).

## 9 Conclusion

We gave an open rule that ties an agent's autonomy on each action type to a run of verified successes sized by an error budget, with a conditional bound on the errors it lets through, and tested it under a pre-registered protocol. On four public benchmarks and three Claude 5.5 models, none of the twelve configurations earned delegation: the only one that could be judged has an error rate established above its bar, and the other eleven lacked the volume to conclude. The rule was not refuted, but it was hardly put to the test, and its usefulness could not be judged. The errors counted are action errors confirmed against a single expected path per task, and 3 of 10 confirmed alerts in the author's blind sample were lifted. The delegation gap is the main descriptive result: the excesses these volumes could establish are large, smaller gaps could not be resolved either way, no type could be shown below its bar, and earning autonomy under explicit budgets takes far more verified actions per action type than these benchmarks provide at our volumes. Code, data, registrations and the exact replay command will be public when the paper is posted (Appendix F).

Resumption uses absolute paths, tested in a dry run, and the launch notes were corrected accordingly.
- **D4 (10 and 14 November): author's lifts outside any written reason.** In wave 1, the author lifted two sheets under reason (d) because the simulated client departed from its own instruction. No frozen reason covers this case ((d) is reserved for defects of our comparison code). Under the frozen rule the author's decision prevails and a reason is never rewritten, so these lifts enter the confirmed verdicts (the engine counts 3 alerts as lifted by the author without a written reason; the third, from wave 2, is not described here; no sensitivity analysis reconfirming these 3 of 2,761 alerts was run; Section 6.1). This points to a real limitation of the benchmarks: a simulated client that drifts makes the agent who follows it look wrong (Section 8).
- **D5 (13 November): cost above the announced interval.** API spend reached 837.19 USD against a preregistered estimate of 595.73 USD (517 to 674 USD at 95 %). Banking cost two to three times more per episode than in the pilot. No pass-count reduction was triggered (Section 6.1).
- **D6 (13 November): tools missing from the tool table** (instrument). The wave-2 report lists 27 banking tool names absent from the frozen tool table: 19 real tools, present in the frozen tool definitions but never mapped, and 8 `_placeholder` names invented by the agent; 2 invented names appear on retail. Their writes stay without a verdict (`unknown`, tier 1) and explain banking's coverage (92.5 % in wave 2; 98.5 % overall in that wave, above the threshold). Nothing was corrected, since the code is frozen.
- **D7 (13–14 November): alert review procedure for wave 2.** The 2,529 wave-2 alerts were reviewed in 1,428 batches by a script outside the frozen perimeter (`outils/relecture_lots.py`), with the wave-1 prompt word for word. Each batch went to a fresh headless Claude Code instance on a subscription (no API key), without the author's personal settings, with the model recorded: Claude Opus 5.5. The wave-1 reviewing model and batching rule were not recorded. Wave-2 batches held at most 120 kB and 15 sheets; all 1,428 closed at the first attempt (about 2 h 05 with six instances in parallel). Reviewers could technically open the alert file, which the prompt forbade; no transcript was kept, so blindness rests on the instruction, not on a technical barrier. Opus 5.5 reviewed episodes played in part by Opus 5.5 (Section 8, limitation 9). The harness of the wave-1 review was not recorded either, so the two waves were not reviewed under documented identical conditions. The analyses suggested by the audit of 14 November (lifts broken down by agent model, a review of a sample by another provider, re-reviewing wave 1 with the wave-2 harness) were not done.
- **D8 (13 November): simplified review page reused in wave 2.** The simplified page of D1 also served for the author's wave-2 blind review, with the four guided questions in the order b, c, a, d and the default "when in doubt, a real error". The page computes the answer from four yes/no questions and invites the reader not to read everything, which may steer the reviewer; both wave-1 disagreements concerned question (d).
- **D9 (14 November): audit of alert-free tasks.** The 20 audit sheets were read by a script outside the frozen perimeter (`outils/audit_relecture.py`): one fresh Opus 5.5 instance per sheet, on the subscription, with the frozen instruction; the model is recorded. Result: 0 removals, 0 invisible errors in 21 writes.
- **D10 (14 November): wave-2 exclusion diagnosis.** Reading only the error lines of the failed attempts' logs: of 226 technical failures in wave 2, 207 came from an empty agent message and 19 from an empty simulated-client message, a simulator failure (in wave 1, all 19 came from the agent). The exploratory analysis fixed in the verdict-freeze deposit counts only the former as errors. Final count of excluded episodes: 91 (Table 3).
- **D11 (14 November): opposition on retail task 98, and a replaced verdict-freeze registration.** A first verdict-freeze registration (OSF u26kr, 06:44:26 UTC) was submitted. An audit by a separate Claude instance then found, before any draw, that retail task 98 had been removed on a consequence-based argument outside the frozen criterion (a recommendation error by the AI assistant, acknowledged), that the exact final-replay command was not fixed anywhere, and that the reference timestamp was ambiguous. The author opposed the removal of task 98, whose 23 alerts lifted for reason (a) became confirmed errors. u26kr could no longer be cancelled: its embargo had already been approved by the author on the assistant's instruction. The author chose to deposit a new registration (OSF cusv6, `date_registered` 2026-11-14T08:17:47 UTC) declaring that it replaces u26kr and is the only one that counts, before the number designated by u26kr existed, then to withdraw u26kr (done). The draw designated by u26kr was never run. The three banking removals based on the knowledge base were kept; reading the policy's pointer to the knowledge base as part of the task's files is an interpretation of the frozen instruction, declared here. No sensitivity analysis keeping these three tasks was run. Banking task_082, removed for case a3, was the least clear-cut removal.
- **D12 (17 November): final replay, a code defect and its in-memory fix.** The final replay launched with the deposited command failed after 4,614 s while writing the stress-test table (`resistance.csv`): the frozen function `lignes_resistance` passed two `mode` keys to the same record, a case that never arose in the pilot because no stress-test cell was judged there. The frozen code was not edited, since the engine refuses to run if the repository differs from the frozen manifest. Instead, a wrapper script (`outils/rejeu_final_correctif.py`) replaced that single function in memory, renaming the second field `mode_scenario`, and called the engine's entry point with exactly the deposited arguments. Strictly speaking, this is therefore not the same command. The run metadata (`meta.json`) records the fingerprint of the frozen `sorties.py` and says nothing about the in-memory replacement; a separate note documents it. The replaced function only formats `resistance.csv`, which therefore has one extra column, and it is called after the label has been computed, so the label does not depend on it. No output of the first run was read before the second run; the six tables written by both runs are byte-identical, as checked by comparing SHA-256 fingerprints only (`revues/2026-11-17_identite_six_tables.md`).
- **D13 (17 November): tools and analyses after the verdict.** Presentation scripts outside the frozen perimeter were corrected or added after an audit of the results: `outils/tables_article.py` (tables 3, 5, 6, 8, 9; only simple derived quantities such as counts, ratios and n_eff × bar, never a new test), `outils/tables_exploratoires.py` and a figure register (`article/sources_chiffres.csv`). The exploratory analyses of Appendix D (`outils/analyses_exploratoires.py`, A to G) were run after the verdict, read the frozen engine's outputs and functions without modifying them, are dated 17 November 2026, and have no effect on the label.
