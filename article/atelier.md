## 1 Introduction

An agent that books flights, refunds orders or files calendar entries takes dozens of actions per task. Most are reads; some change the state of the world. The question every deployment faces is: *which of these actions can the agent take alone, and on what evidence?* Current practice answers with fixed allow-lists, with a risk triage that ignores the agent's record, or with trust that grows without a stated criterion. None ties the decision to the agent's observed record on that kind of action, and none says how many errors the arrangement lets through.

We study an alternative from industrial quality control. In a continuous sampling plan (Dodge, 1943, p. 265), every unit is inspected until i consecutive units conform; inspection then drops to a fraction and returns to 100 % at the first defect. Read in reverse for agents: validate every action of a type until the agent has N consecutive verified successes on it, then let it act alone on that type, and withdraw autonomy at the first error. Without any assumption of statistical control, such a plan bounds outgoing quality (Lieberman, 1953, pp. 480-482; Derman, Johns and Lieberman, 1959, pp. 1176-1177), and a cycle argument gives a conditional bound on the extra errors that delegation lets through (detected errors, immediate detection, a write mix close to the expected one; Section 3).

**Contributions.** (1) A complete, open rule for earned autonomy *per action type*, whose run length N is derived from an error budget fixed in advance, with a conditional bound on the extra errors (Section 3). (2) A measurement of the *delegation gap*: for each model, environment and action type observed, the confirmed error rate against the bar the budget requires, with its interval; half of these types remain undetermined (Section 6.4). (3) A pre-registered protocol, in which, at our volumes, only types with enough effective writes could decide the label; one did (Section 6.2). The plan stated in advance that at these volumes no type could be certified below its bar and that the high-stakes run lengths exceed the data: plan, rule, code, seeds and labels were registered on OSF on 10 November 2026 at 05:00 UTC, before wave 1 started at 05:19 UTC; alerts are reviewed on blind sheets by separate Claude instances, and a blind sample by the author; the calibration/confirmation split is drawn from a public randomness beacon after the confirmed verdicts are frozen.

**Results in brief.** None of the twelve configurations reached the pre-registered level of evidence required for delegation (label R4). Only Haiku on WorkBench had enough volume to be judged: its reversible writes have a confirmed action-error rate of 8.6 % on the calibration half against a 0.76 % bar (48 of its 79 errors are one plot end-date discrepancy that the reviewers decided both ways; lifting it throughout leaves 3.4 %, established by a two-error margin; Section 6.7) (errors against a single expected path per task; Haiku's task success on WorkBench is 95 % to 97 % per half, and 64 % of its episodes with a confirmed error over the whole reserve are successful tasks). Descriptively, of 40 observed model–environment–type combinations, 20 have an error rate established above their bar (11 of them on banking, where agent and setup errors cannot be separated), 13 show no observed error but too little volume, and none could be shown below its bar at these volumes. The safety criterion was not refuted, but with almost no power; utility could not be judged.

## 2 Related work

*Quality control.* Continuous sampling plans (Dodge, 1943), their tolerant (Dodge and Torrey, 1951, as reported by Hryniewicz, 2011), multi-level (Lieberman and Solomon, 1955) and tightened (Derman, Littauer and Solomon, 1957) variants, and the unrestricted AOQL (Lieberman, 1953; Derman, Johns and Lieberman, 1959) provide our template; Wald and Wolfowitz (1945, p. 47) had noted that Dodge's AOQL formulas need statistical control, and Hillier (1964) proposed a limit on extra defectives after a jump. Our bound adapts the UAOQL cycle argument to a check that follows execution; we do not claim it as new. *Levels of autonomy.* Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens (2000) and Feng, McDonald and Zhang (2025) define levels without a statistical mechanism for moving between them; Weber and Taneja (2026) promote per skill on windows of N outputs and a correction-rate threshold, with no budget-derived N or bound. *Error budgets.* CASE (Telukunta, Lilis and Baron, 2026) makes autonomy a non-decreasing function of the remaining error budget (Eq. 7, p. 10) and already speaks of autonomy "earned against a measured error budget" (p. 11); the function is unspecified and untested. *Earned authority.* Zhang and Zhang (2026) formalise authority that falls freely, rises only under an abstract evidence relation and stays below a user-fixed ceiling; we instantiate that relation and put it to a pre-registered test, which had little power (Section 6.3). *Statistics of evaluations.* We follow Miller (2024): the task is the independent unit, rules are compared on the same traces, and intervals come from a task-level jackknife-t; simultaneous inference uses max-T (Westfall and Young, 1993). The extended version of this paper (`article/brouillon.md` in the code archive; see Appendix F) gives the full related work, including selective classification, AI control and agent benchmarks.

## 3 The rule

**Action types and caps.** A frozen table maps each benchmark tool to a type, a stakes level and reversibility: low (read, reversible write ECR, internal message MSG), medium (durable write DUR, execution), high (external output, money ARG, guardrail or rights GAR, illegible effect ILL). Evidence stays within its type. Reads are autonomous and uncounted; the budget is counted per write. Some actions are always validated (caps): guardrails and rights, consent, secrets, money above a per-environment amount, a first message to an unknown recipient. The idea of a ceiling that evidence cannot lift follows Zhang and Zhang (2026).

**Tiers and B-all.** Tier 1: *ask before*; tier 2: *act then notify* (reversible types); tier 3: *act alone*. Under the main rule, **B-all** (named B-tout in the frozen plan), a type reaches tier 3 after N consecutive verified successes; any error resets the counter; an error at tier 3 withdraws autonomy (to tier 1 with a trial at medium and high stakes); a model change resets the counter. Below tier 3, tier 2 follows a Wald (1945) sequential score. An automated check judges each write; an alert counts as an error only after confirmation.

**From a budget to N.** The author fixed, blind and in advance, the extra errors he would accept relative to "validate everything": 0.5, 0.05 and 0.005 per 100 writes at low, medium and high stakes. The budget becomes a per-type *bar*, bar = B/(0.95 × 100 × q), where q is the expected share of the level's writes, and N = ⌈1/(d × bar)⌉ − 1 with d = 0.95 the assumed detection share. N ranges from 12 to 138 at low stakes, 204 to 733 at medium and 2,582 to 12,653 at high stakes (Appendix B, Table 1).

**Conditional guarantee.** Each autonomy period follows at least N verified successes and ends at the first detected error. Counting in cycles, the expected number of errors executed at tier 3 per write of the type is at most 1/(1 + N d), whatever the error sequence; with immediate and complete detection, at most one executed error per N + 1 writes. At a level, the extra errors stay below 0.95 × budget × s/q, where s is the real share of the level's writes. The bound assumes immediate detection of the errors the check sees and misses that are random rather than targeted; it is a long-run bound when d < 1 (Section B.3.4).

## 4 Setup

**Benchmarks and models.** Four public environments: τ-bench 2.0 airline, retail and banking (Yao et al., 2024; Barres et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2026), and WorkBench (Styles et al., 2024), graded without any LLM judge; 20 % of tasks served for a pilot and never enter the verdict. Agents: Claude Haiku, Sonnet and Opus 5.5 with default settings; the τ-bench simulated user is Haiku 5.5. A *configuration* is one model in one environment (12). 14,266 episodes were planned (Haiku 50 passes on airline and retail, 10 on banking, 5 on WorkBench; Sonnet and Opus 10, 10, 3 and 1).

**Instrument.** Every write made with a tool of the frozen table is judged by exact comparison with the task's expected actions. Every alert (a write marked wrong, duplicate or refused) is reviewed by fresh Claude instances on blind sheets and can be lifted only for one of four pre-written reasons with proof: defective task, rephrasing of a free-text field, another path allowed by the policy, or a defect of our code. In each wave the author blind-reviews 15 alerts; at least 13 agreements keep the review.

**Halves, draw and replay.** The reserve is split by task into a *calibration* half, which measures each configuration's error rate by type and decides delegability, and a *confirmation* half, where the frozen rule is replayed once. The split was drawn from a NIST beacon pulse after the confirmed verdicts were frozen and registered (OSF cusv6). The rule and its comparators ("validate everything", a fixed list, a risk triage) are replayed offline on the recorded episodes with a simulated validator that misses 5 % of errors, 2,000 replays per configuration (Appendix B).

## 5 Pre-registered analysis

**Labels.** Point 0 checks the instrument (coverage ≥ 0.95, blind review kept). H1 (safety, eliminatory): B-all has no established budget excess (max-T) in any of 180 cells (12 configurations and 48 model-change sequences × 3 stakes levels); a failure gives R1. A configuration is *delegable* if at least one type has a median calibration rate at most its bar; a type is *judgeable* if zero errors would bring it under its bar ($n_{\mathrm{eff}} \times \mathrm{bar}$ at least about ln 2, 0.6905 at the WorkBench low-stakes bar), where $n_{\mathrm{eff}} = n/(1 + (\bar m - 1)\hat\rho)$ is the effective number of the type's $n$ writes under the estimated within-task correlation $\hat\rho$ ($\bar m$: writes per task, weighted). A rate is *established above* its bar when its lower bound lies above it. If no configuration is delegable, the label is R4 when at least one type is judgeable and every configuration with a judgeable type has one whose one-sided 95 % Clopper–Pearson lower bound at ρ = 1 lies above its bar; otherwise "insufficient evidence". R4's registered name is "nothing to delegate: unreliability established"; we keep the label, not the name, as a description, since what is established concerns one action type in one configuration (Section 6.2). H2 (utility) compares loads over delegable configurations; it is not judged under R4. Order: R5, R1, insufficient evidence, R4, R2, confirmed.

**What the labels can mean.** With immediate detection the bound holds for any sequence, so H1 can hardly fail through the data; R1 could mean a mix gap (s above about 1.05 × q), not that agents are unreliable. The part that can really fail is H2, together with the delegation gap. Lower bounds are per type, without multiplicity correction, under the effective-size approximation (Appendix C).

## 6 Results

**Collection and instrument (6.1).** All 14,266 planned episodes were played; 91 were excluded, all for an empty agent message that τ-bench records as an infrastructure error (77 Haiku on banking); 13,623 are analysed after exclusions and removal of 12 defective tasks. These exclusions remove agent failures and favour delegation. Instrument coverage was 98.7 % (banking 92.5 % to 93.0 %). 2,761 alerts were reviewed: 1,991 confirmed and 770 lifted. The author's blind review agreed 13 of 15 times in each wave, exactly the threshold; three of the four disagreements lifted a confirmed alert, and the pre-registered re-review of all lifted high-stakes alerts was not triggered. API spend was 837.19 USD, above the pre-registered estimate (517 to 674 USD). Appendix C gives the full account.

**Label (6.2).** No configuration reached the pre-registered level of evidence required for delegation (label R4). Only one of twelve had enough volume to be judged (Haiku on WorkBench, reversible writes): its confirmed error rate, 8.6 % (914 writes, 79 errors; lower bound 4.1 % at ρ = 1), is established above its 0.76 % bar. The other eleven have no judgeable type. Judgeability is narrow and rests on the estimated correlation: $n_{\mathrm{eff}} \times \mathrm{bar} = 0.740$ against 0.6905 at ρ̂ = 0.819 ($n_{\mathrm{eff}} = 97.7$); at ρ = 1 ($n_{\mathrm{eff}} = 81.6$, product 0.618) the type would not be judgeable, and above ρ̂ = 0.885 the label would become "insufficient evidence" (a post hoc computation; Appendix D gives an approximate bootstrap interval for ρ̂, with a simpler estimator than the engine's). The excess is established at ρ = 1; the judgeability that makes it count is not. Raw verdicts and the role swap also give R4, but they sit at the edge of the same threshold.

**Safety (6.3).** Point 1 is held in the sense of "not refuted", with almost no power: no established excess, but an excess would have needed an estimate more than seven standard errors above the budget (c* = 7.0), and no cell is demonstrated below budget. Of 180 cells, 51 were judged (all at low stakes), 108 held without being put to the test and 21 were without object. Central estimates stay at most 0.38 times the budget (Figure 1). In 24 judged cells (WorkBench and banking) s/q exceeds 1/0.95, so the construction bound alone does not guarantee the budget there.

**Delegation gap (6.4).** Descriptively and without multiplicity correction, 20 of the 40 model–environment–type combinations are above their bar at ρ = 1, 20 undetermined, none below; at these volumes the table could only return "above" or "undetermined". Of the 20, 11 are on banking, 6 are money-moving writes on airline and retail, and 3 are reversible writes on WorkBench. The 13 types without observed errors have 3 to 122 writes. On WorkBench, reversible writes err 5.5 % to 8.6 % against a 0.76 % bar (lower bound ÷ bar 2.7 to 5.5), with similar rates on the confirmation half (5.9 % to 11.0 %). Money-moving writes on airline and retail err 6.5 % to 13.3 % on the calibration half against bars near 0.008 % (Figure 2), but 1.2 % to 7.7 % on the confirmation half (3.8 % to 8.0 % on the whole reserve): the two halves differ by up to a factor of five, and the intervals are wide (upper bounds 20 % to 51 %). At such bars a single error in 500 writes already gives a ratio of about 20: the high-stakes ratios mostly measure the budget chosen. Banking rates are higher (28 % to 56 % for money-moving and medium-stakes writes), but banking is where attribution to the agent is weakest (coverage 0.925, unmapped tools, a knowledge base not shown to reviewers, high stakes carried by the simulated client, 89 of 91 exclusions). On the whole reserve (descriptive), three configurations would have had a delegable type, all with zero observed errors (Appendix C, Table 6 bis).

**Utility and model changes (6.5–6.6).** Point 2 is not judged under R4, and "validate everything" is not beaten. B-all asked for 55 to 66 validations per 100 non-read actions on WorkBench, mostly through tier 2 on a type established above its bar, where about 4.6 to 6.2 errors per 100 writes of that type executed before being flagged (exploratory), and 84 to 100 elsewhere (Figure 3). The fixed list and the risk triage have established excesses in one and two configurations (all Haiku, low stakes): an illustration of the gap, not a success of B-all, since they lack error feedback and tier 2. At medium and high stakes no autonomy followed any model change, so the withdrawal criterion passed untested; at low stakes, in the secondary reading (point estimate above the bar), withdrawal after a silent change took 40 to 61 writes on retail, with one error let through on average. Types above their bar still reached tier 3 in some replays (banking/Opus internal messages: 4.3 %, up to 11.8 % after a silent change).

**Secondary analyses (6.7).** In the main analysis, secondary rules had no established excess (B-regular had 4 under the delay10 stress test); the stress-test alert fired in all seven modes, driven by one airline cell carried by 2 tasks (worst upper bound 1.65 to 1.68 times the budget, 2.34 under delay10, 0 failures). Other secondary outputs (approve everything, three successes, oracle, order of episodes) are in Appendix C. Exploratory analyses, dated after the verdict (Appendix D): counting the 91 exclusions as errors adds one established type; the judgeable type stays above its bar with up to 63 % false confirmations (Section 7). A second exploratory pass (Appendix D, Tables X7 and X8, dated 18 November 2026) read every confirmed plot error (`analytics.create_plot`) of the judged type: 56 of its 79 errors are plots, and 48 of them differ from the expected action only by the end date (the agent plots up to 30 November, "today", where the benchmark expects 29 November). Across the study, the reviewing instances decided that end-date case both ways (177 such plot alerts lifted, 144 confirmed, all models and both halves). Lifting it throughout leaves 31 errors (3.4 %, lower bound 0.86 %), still established, but by two errors (29 are needed); lifting also four plots whose window is shifted by one day would leave 27, not established, and the label would become "insufficient evidence". The label R4 therefore depends on how one recurring, arguably acceptable discrepancy is judged.

## 7 Discussion

R4 does not say that the agents are unreliable in general, that nothing could be delegated, or which model is better: it rests on one configuration, and only Haiku's volume made a type judgeable. Task success and action correctness also disagree: on WorkBench, 64 % to 82 % of episodes with a confirmed action error are successful tasks, and in every environment 47 % to 77 % of failed episodes have no confirmed action error (Table X7, exploratory). Failed episodes without a confirmed action error fail through reads, omissions or answers, which the action check does not count; on WorkBench, most errors inside successful tasks are the plot end-date case of Section 6.7, so this gap partly reflects the expected-path convention. What bound the study was volume, not the bound. At the budgets we fixed, the evidence the rule demands is out of reach at medium and high stakes for one benchmark configuration, and at low stakes for agents that err several percent of the time. Table X2 (exploratory) gives, for each established excess, the share of confirmed errors that could be false alerts before the excess is lost: 70 % to 79 % on retail money-moving writes, 31 % to 55 % on airline, 63 % for the type that carries R4. The author's blind sample (3 of 10 confirmed alerts lifted, five per wave; 95 % interval 7 % to 65 %, all environments pooled) is too small to rule such shares out: at its upper end, 9 of the 20 established excesses, including the one that carries R4, would no longer be established. Zhang and Zhang leave the evidence relation abstract; our rule is one instance; the budgets fix what it costs (Table 1), and our data show that agents erring several percent of the time could not pay it at these volumes. CASE ties autonomy to the remaining budget; our N is a different way, sized in advance per action type with a stated bound, and our data say nothing about CASE's mechanism. In practice, no type could have been delegated with evidence: 13 had no observed error but too few writes, 7 had errors without an established excess, and 20 had established excesses. Earning autonomy under such budgets takes tens to hundreds of verified successes at low stakes and thousands to tens of thousands at high stakes. A tier where actions execute then get flagged lowers load but lets errors through (about 4.6 to 6.2 errors per 100 writes of the type on WorkBench, exploratory). The extended version of this paper (see Appendix F) gives the full discussion.

## 8 Limitations

Offline replay of fixed traces with a simulated validator; immediate detection; one expected path per task; tasks replayed up to 50 times; a simulated user that may drift; same-provider reviewers, blind by instruction only, with the wave-1 reviewing model not recorded and wave-2 batches closed in about 32 s each; a blind sample of 10 confirmed alerts, too small to bound false confirmations; an inconsistent review of a recurring plot end-date discrepancy that carries 48 of the 79 errors of the judged type, on which the label depends (Table X8); exclusions that favour delegation; insufficient volume at medium and high stakes; refused writes counted as errors; confirmed errors inside successful tasks (64 % to 82 % of the WorkBench episodes with a confirmed error); an audit of alert-free tasks too small to be informative (0 of 21 writes); one provider, three models, four environments. Appendix E lists all 32 limitations, each marked as pre-registered or added after the results, and Appendix A lists the 14 deviations from the pre-registration (D0 to D13), including an in-memory fix of a code defect in the final replay and the replacement of a verdict-freeze registration (OSF u26kr by cusv6).

## 9 Conclusion

On four public benchmarks and three Claude 5.5 models, none of the twelve configurations earned delegation: the only one that could be judged has an error rate established above its bar, and the other eleven lacked the effective volume to conclude. The rule was not refuted, but it was hardly put to the test, and its usefulness could not be judged. The errors counted are action errors confirmed against a single expected path per task, and 3 of 10 confirmed alerts in the author's blind sample were lifted. The main descriptive result is the delegation gap: the excesses these volumes could establish are large (lower bound 2.7 to 5.5 times the bar on WorkBench reversible writes), smaller gaps could not be resolved either way, and no type could be shown below its bar. As the plan stated in advance, earning autonomy under explicit budgets takes far more verified actions per action type than these benchmarks provide at our volumes. Code, data, registrations and the exact replay command will be public when the paper is posted (Appendix F).
